Should governments monitor internet usage?

State surveillance can help fight crime and terrorism — but it also threatens privacy, free speech and trust in democratic institutions. Both sides below.

Arguments for monitoring Arguments against monitoring

+ For monitoring

🛡 National security

  • Detect terrorist planning and extremist networks
  • Prevent attacks before they happen
  • Protect critical infrastructure from cyber threats

⚖️ Crime prevention

  • Investigate fraud, trafficking and child exploitation
  • Trace criminals who hide behind anonymous accounts
  • Support police with digital evidence in court

🌍 Public safety online

  • Reduce spread of illegal content (CSAM, weapons sales)
  • Identify coordinated disinformation campaigns
  • Respond faster to cyberbullying and harassment cases

📋 Legal compliance

  • Companies already collect data; law sets clear rules
  • Court warrants can limit abuse of power
  • Citizens expect protection in a digital society

− Against monitoring

🔐 Privacy rights

  • People should control their own personal data
  • Mass surveillance treats all citizens as suspects
  • Private messages and browsing habits are sensitive

🗣 Free speech

  • People self-censor if they know they are watched
  • Journalists and activists need confidential sources
  • Political dissent may be suppressed unfairly

⚠️ Abuse of power

  • Authoritarian states use surveillance against opponents
  • Data leaks expose innocent people's information
  • Rules may be ignored without strong oversight

🎯 Limited effectiveness

  • Criminals use encryption and dark web tools
  • Too much data makes it hard to find real threats
  • Resources better spent on targeted investigations

A common middle path is targeted monitoring with judicial warrants and independent oversight — not unrestricted mass surveillance of every citizen.

Sample essay — PTE format (discuss both views)

Should governments monitor internet usage?

As more of daily life moves online, governments face pressure to monitor digital activity for security reasons. While some people support state surveillance to prevent crime and protect citizens, others believe that internet monitoring violates privacy and threatens freedom. This essay will discuss both views and give my own opinion.

On the one hand, there are valid reasons for limited government monitoring. First, it can improve national security. Intelligence agencies may detect terrorist plots or extremist recruitment online before attacks occur. For example, encrypted messaging has been used to coordinate violence in several countries, and lawful investigation can save lives. Second, monitoring helps fight serious crime. Police can trace fraudsters, human traffickers and distributors of child abuse material who operate through websites and social platforms. Third, the public expects safety in a digital society. Just as CCTV exists in public spaces, many citizens accept some online oversight if it is transparent and controlled by law. Finally, court-approved warrants can balance security with rules, so monitoring is not completely unlimited.

On the other hand, widespread internet surveillance raises serious concerns. The main objection is the right to privacy. Browsing history, private messages and location data reveal intimate details of a person's life. Mass collection of this information treats innocent citizens like suspects. Another problem is the effect on free speech. When people know they are being watched, they may avoid criticising the government or discussing sensitive topics. Journalists and whistleblowers need protection, not constant monitoring. In addition, power can be abused. In authoritarian regimes, surveillance tools are used to silence opposition and track activists. Even in democracies, data breaches have exposed millions of records. Finally, blanket monitoring may be ineffective, because serious criminals often use encryption, while huge volumes of ordinary data distract investigators from real threats.

In conclusion, governments have a duty to protect citizens from crime and terrorism online, but they must not destroy privacy and free expression in the process. In my opinion, targeted monitoring with strict legal limits is acceptable, but mass surveillance of all internet users is not. Independent courts, clear warrants and oversight bodies are essential so that security measures remain proportionate and trustworthy.
